Theory
======

In this section the background theory of the project is explained. Detailed references for specific formulas are included in the code itself.

Wave Spectra
##############
Wave spectra are defined such that the integral of the spectrum is the variance of the surface elevation [Journée]_ (5.111) & [Journée]_ (5.116):

.. math::
    \int_{0}^{\infty}S(\omega)d\omega = \sigma ^{2} = {H_{s}}^{2}/16

or in discrete form:

.. math::
    \sum_{i=0}^{N_{freqs}}S(\omega_{i})\Delta\omega = \sigma ^{2} = {H_{s}}^{2}/16

where :math:`H_{s}` is the significant wave height and :math:`\sigma` is the standard deviation of the surface elevation.


By choosing the discrete Frequency points such that the energy of each discrete part is constant :math:`S(\omega)\Delta\omega = C_S` the equation is simplified to:

.. math::
    N_{freqs} \cdot C_S = \sigma ^{2} = {H_{s}}^{2}/16


The equal energy frequency points are calculated by numerically integrating the wave spectrum.


Wave Directional spreading
***************************

Because vessel motions depend on wave direction, the wave spectrum must be direction-dependent. To simulate that waves also vary with direction, the wave spectrum is multiplied by a directionality function :math:`D(\theta)`:

.. math::
    S(\omega, \theta) = S(\omega) \cdot D(\theta)

This must be done without changing the total energy of the wave spectrum,

.. math::
    \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}D(\theta)d\theta = 1

By choosing the directions such that the energy of each discrete part is constant :math:`D(\theta)\Delta\theta = C_d` the equation is simplified to:

.. math::
    N_{dirs} \cdot C_d = 1

A commonly used directionality function is the cosine function [DNV]_ (3.5.8.4):

.. math::
    D(\theta) = \frac{\Gamma(1+n/2)}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(1/2 + n/2)} \cdot \cos^{n}(\theta - \theta_p)

This function describes how wave energy is spread around the main wave direction :math:`\theta_p`. A spreading function is used to account for the fact that, even if we specify a main wave direction, sea states are not perfectly unidirectional and energy is spread over a range of directions around the main direction.

The spreading parameter :math:`n` defines how narrow or wide the spreading is. DNV recommends the following:

- For wind sea, typical values are :math:`n=2` to :math:`n=4`
- For swell, :math:`n \geq 6` is more appropriate

Choosing the Wave Spectra
**************************

Several wave spectra exist to represent different sea states and different geographical locations. Before going through which spectra to choose in PyBM, it is important to understand that wave spectra are statistical representations of the sea surface elevation.
They attempt to describe the ocean state in a certain area using simplified and ideal assumptions. An actual ocean wave spectrum will be much more complicated and variable. For example, it may have two peaks, one from distance swell and the other generated by local wind.
Therefore, it is important to understand the limitations of each wave spectra and choose the one that best represents the sea state of interest.

The following wave spectra are implemented in PyBM:

Pierson-Moskowitz
------------------

The Pierson-Moskowitz (PM) spectrum is frequently applied for fully-developed seas with predominantly wind wave systems. It essentially assumes that the waves are generated by local wind blowing steadily over a large area for a long time, leading to a fully developed sea state. 
Therefore, this spectrum is suited for open ocean conditions with long fetch and duration (e.g. Atlantic Ocean).

JONSWAP
---------

The JONSWAP (Joint North Sea Wave Project) spectrum is an enhancement of the PM spectrum, developed based on measurements in the North Sea. It introduces a peak enhancement factor (:math:`\gamma`) to better represent the sharper peak observed in the wave spectra of developing seas.
The choice of :math:`\gamma` affects the sharpness of the peak, with higher values leading to a more pronounced peak. Typical values for :math:`\gamma` range from 1 (equivalent to PM) to 7. DNV proposes that the following values can be used (implemented as default in PyBM):

.. math::
    \gamma = 
    \begin{cases} 
    5 & \text{for } T_{p}/{\sqrt{H_{s}}} \leq 3.6 \\
    \exp(5.75 - 1.15 T_{p}/{\sqrt{H_{s}}}) & \text{for } 3.6 < T_{p}/{\sqrt{H_{s}}} < 5 \\
    1 & \text{for } 5 \leq T_{p}/{\sqrt{H_{s}}}
    \end{cases}

This spectrum is more suitable for fetch-limited and duration-limited sea states, such as those found in enclosed seas. However, this spectrum is also widely used for open ocean conditions.

According to [Mazzaretto]_, the JONSWAP spectrum did not perform well in areas such as Northern Brazil, coast from Mauritania to Nigeria, India, West coast of Central America, Northern West Australia, East coast of Africa, and West coast of Madagascar.

Torsethaugen
-----------------

The Torsethaugen spectrum is a double peaked spectrum that can represent sea states that include both a remotely generated swell and local wind-generated waves. 
It fits two generalized JONSWAP functions. It can be used in areas where single-peak spectra do not perform well, for example in coastal areas. According to [ABS]_ notes, it is best suited to North Sea conditions.


Motion Analysis
###############

Motion analysis is based on multiplication of vessel motion RAOs with wave spectra.

Frequency Domain Response
*************************

The response of a vessel can be calculated using:

.. math::
    {\sigma_{r}}^{2} = \sum_{i=0}^{N_{freqs}}|RAO_{i}|^{2}S(\omega_{i})\Delta\omega_i

For equal energy discrete frequency points this is simplified to:

.. math::
    {\sigma_r}^{2} = \frac{{H_s}^2}{16 \cdot N_{freqs}} \sum_{i=0}^{N_{freqs}}|RAO_{i}|^{2}

Where :math:`\sigma_{r}` is the standard deviation of the response.

In general, the spectral moment of the response is calculated using:

.. math::
    m_{n} = \frac{{H_s}^2}{16 \cdot N_{freqs}} \sum_{i=0}^{N_{freqs}}|RAO_{i}|^{2}{\omega_i}^n

When directional spreading is included, the response is calculated using:

.. math::
    {\sigma_{r}}^{2} = \sum_{i=0}^{N_{freqs}}\sum_{j=0}^{N_{dirs}}|RAO_{i,j}|^{2}S(\omega_{i})D(\theta_{j})\Delta\omega_i\Delta\theta_j

For equal energy discrete frequency and direction points this is simplified to:

.. math::
    {\sigma_r}^{2} = \frac{{H_s}^2}{16 \cdot N_{freqs} \cdot N_{dirs}} \sum_{i=0}^{N_{freqs}}\sum_{j=0}^{N_{dirs}}|RAO_{i,j}|^{2}


Most Probable Extreme
----------------------

The most probable extreme is calculated using the following formula [Journée]_ (11.20):

.. math::
    MPE = \sigma_r \cdot \sqrt{2 \cdot \ln(\frac{T}{T_z})}

Where :math:`T` is the is storm duration (typically 3 hours) and :math:`T_z` is the zero-crossing period.
The zero-crossing period is given by [Journée]_ (6.84)

.. math::
    T_z = 2\pi \cdot \sqrt{\frac{m_0}{m_2}}

Time Domain Response
*********************

Single direction
----------------

A response time trace of the vessel is obtained with the sum of regular waves for each frequency component [Perez]_ (4.32):

.. math::
    \xi_r(t) = \sum_{i=0}^{N_{freqs}} A_i \cdot \cos(\omega_i \cdot t + \phi_i)

Where :math:`\phi_{i}` is a randomly generated phase for each frequency component. It should be noted that the same phase is used
for all DOFs of the vessel to preserve the phase difference between DOFs.

:math:`A_i` is the amplitude of the response for each frequency component and for equal energy frequency points is calculated using:

.. math::
    {A_i}^2 = \frac{{2 \cdot H_s}^2}{16 \cdot N_{freqs}} \cdot {|RAO_i|}^2

Where :math:`RAO_i` is the RAO for the frequency component :math:`i`.

Multiple directions
-------------------

When multiple directions are considered, :math:`N_{freqs} \cdot N_{dirs}` time traces are generated and the total response is obtained by summing all time traces:
To prevent correlation between frequencies of different directions, different frequency points are used for each direction [OrcaFlex]_. Therefore at total of :math:`N_{freqs} \cdot N_{dirs}`
equal energy frequency points are generated, and distributed over the different directions.

RAO Translation
****************

To obtain the response of the vessel at a different point of interest (POI), the RAO is translated before computing the response.
This ensures that the phases between degrees of freedom (DOF) are accounted for. The translated RAO at point :math:`[p_x, p_y, p_z]` is obtained using [Perez]_ (4.27):

.. math::
    \begin{align*}
    RAO_x = RAO_x + p_z \cdot RAO_{ry} - p_y \cdot RAO_{rz} \\
    RAO_y = RAO_y + p_x \cdot RAO_{rz} - p_z \cdot RAO_{rx} \\
    RAO_z = RAO_z + p_y \cdot RAO_{rx} - p_x \cdot RAO_{ry}
    \end{align*}

Where :math:`RAO_{d}` is the RAO for the DOF :math:`d`.
